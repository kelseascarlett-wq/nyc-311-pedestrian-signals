An exploratory investigation

Waiting for the walk signal

How long NYC 311 pedestrian-signal reports remain open, by neighborhood.

By Kelsea Scarlett · October 2026

I began with a suspicion that a closed 311 complaint might conceal an unfixed problem. My roommate, a lifelong New Yorker who trusts 311, challenged that assumption and suggested a more precise question.

How long do pedestrian-signal reports remain open in New York City’s 311 system, and how does the typical closure time vary across neighborhoods?

Pedestrians, disability advocates, neighborhood organizations, and journalists could use the results to identify patterns worth investigating.

The data

I used NYC Open Data’s 311 Service Requests from 2020 to Present, published by NYC311 from reports submitted by the public. Each row is one request—not necessarily one broken signal, repair, or unique intersection. I selected 2025 requests assigned to DOT with the complaint type Traffic Signal Condition and descriptor Pedestrian Signal.

My principal calculated variable was closure time: Closed Date minus Created Date. I used the median for geographic comparisons because a small number of extremely long cases pulled the average upward.

To compare places consistently, I used neighborhood-sized areas drawn by NYC’s Department of City Planning. The city calls them Neighborhood Tabulation Areas, or NTAs. The borders are imperfect, but they provide one consistent geography across all five boroughs.

I compared only areas with at least 20 requests so that a handful of cases would not distort the ranking.

Where closure times differ

Recorded closure times varied across the city. The map shows where the differences were concentrated; the ranked bars make their magnitude easier to compare.

Which neighborhoods stand apart

The citywide median administrative closure time was approximately 2.4 hours, but neighborhood medians varied sharply. Brownsville had the longest median at 53.6 hours across 26 complaints. It was followed by East Flatbush-Farragut at 29.6 hours across 22 complaints and Ridgewood at 29.4 hours across 23 complaints. These are differences in the timing of recorded closures; by themselves, they do not demonstrate unequal repair service or intentional preferential treatment.

How long individual cases remained open

The third chart shows the full distribution behind the averages. More than half of requests were marked closed within three hours, while 10.2 percent took more than five days—including 2.9 percent that took more than 30 days. Those older cases raised the mean to about 84 hours even though the median was 2.4 hours.

What the data cannot tell us

A 311 Closed Date marks when the city closed its record, not when a signal was inspected or repaired. The dataset contains no inspection finding or repair outcome. I also excluded 238 requests that lacked enough location information to assign a neighborhood.

Reporting behavior, urgency, duplicates, contractor workflow, and data entry could affect the pattern. The neighborhood charts combine many intersections into one number; the original street fields still allow particular intersections to be examined.

Conclusion

NYC marked the typical request closed within hours, but the timing varied by neighborhood and a minority remained open for days or months. This analysis establishes where recorded closures differed, not why.

One concrete lead is Rockaway Avenue and Sutter Avenue in Brownsville, where two 2025 requests remained open for approximately 103 and 124 days. The next step is to request DOT’s inspection, contractor, and repair records for those cases and compare their dates with the 311 record. A broader public-safety study could then compare complaint locations with police-reported crashes to ask whether intersections with repeated or long-open signal complaints also recorded pedestrian injuries. A geographic match could identify specific intersections for further investigation.
